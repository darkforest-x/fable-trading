# A stop at the wick's own extreme buys a high payoff ratio with the whole edge

- **Problem**: The owner wanted a higher payoff version of live-wick: keep the same entry (market at the touch 5m close) but put the stop just past that 5m bar's low (long) or high (short) instead of 20 percent from the entry.
- **Dead ends**:
  - Expecting the payoff ratio to carry the strategy. It did rise, from 0.97 to 4.4 (mean win 537bp vs mean loss 122bp). But the win rate fell from 67 to 16 percent, and the mean per trade went from +170bp to −14bp. 344 of 416 trades went back through the wick extreme before reaching the target. Wider buffers of 0.3 and 1 percent, or holding a day without the 60m check, were all around 0 to −16bp.
  - Blaming the worse-of-two-intrabar-paths rule. Only 14–17 of 419 rows differ between the paths. Even taking the better path on every row, the wick stop makes +1 to +11bp, against +157bp for E20.
- **What worked (as diagnosis)**: Look at the stop distance against the cost of one round trip. The median wick stop is 61bp from the entry, and 27 percent are under 30bp. Taker fees plus 10bp slippage on entry and exit come to about 30bp, so a stopped trade loses about 1.4R and the cost alone is half the stop. The live-wick edge comes from sitting through the retest of the wick, which the earlier stop studies also showed (see [a-tighter-disaster-stop-sells-the-wick-bottom.md](a-tighter-disaster-stop-sells-the-wick-bottom.md)).
- **General rule**: Before turning a wick-reversal entry into a tight structural-stop trade, check two numbers: how often the winners revisit the wick extreme, and the median stop distance against round-trip cost. A high payoff ratio with a win rate below about 1/(1+payoff) after costs has negative expectancy. A high payoff design needs a different entry, such as a limit order nearer the extreme, not just a tighter stop on the same close entry.
